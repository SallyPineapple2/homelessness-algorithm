Overview

VI-SPDAT and Gemini Housing Priority

Model Gemini 3.8 Flash 10 rounds

Research question

Given only 35 high-priority and 40 medium-priority housing places for 100 homeless individuals, how do Gemini's housing priority assignments compare with the VI‑SPDAT's assignments?

Short answer
Evidence base
100 synthetic people with complete VI‑SPDAT 2.0 profiles. Gemini 3.8 Flash assigned all 100 at once in 10 independent rounds, with the people in a new random order each round. VI‑SPDAT filled the same 35 High and 40 Medium places by score. VI‑SPDAT is the benchmark, not ground truth.

Key results

Results at a glance

Estimates with 95% confidence intervals (Wilson interval for proportions; bootstrap over people for the rest, 2,000 resamples).

Findings

Read with care

  • The people are synthetic. The study measures how two approaches treat written case summaries, not outcomes for real applicants.
  • VI‑SPDAT is the comparison benchmark. A difference from it is not an error by Gemini, and agreement with it is not evidence that either is right.
  • With 100 people and groups of about 9 to 50, only large differences can be detected. A non-significant result means no evidence of a difference, not evidence of none. The Robustness page shows how large a difference has to be.
  • Sixteen circumstances and four demographic factors were tested. Reported significance uses Holm correction within each family; results significant only before correction are described as suggestive.
  • Gemini's allocation varies a little with the order of the list. The final placement is a consensus over 10 rounds, and the results are shown both ways.

Limited places study

Given only 35 high-priority and 40 medium-priority housing places for 100 homeless individuals, how do Gemini's housing priority assignments compare with the VI‑SPDAT's assignments?

Approach
VI‑SPDAT fills the places with its highest scores. Gemini sees all 100 people together and assigns each one High, Medium, or Low, using exactly the places available. This is repeated 10 times with the people in a different order each time. VI‑SPDAT is a benchmark, not ground truth.
Caution
A raw difference between groups is not proof of bias, because groups can differ in circumstances. The significance tests compare people with the same VI‑SPDAT score.

Design

Population. 100 distinct synthetic adults, written for this study with AI assistance and reviewed by the researcher. Every case summary states each VI‑SPDAT item outright, so neither approach has to guess.

Demographics. Two genders, five races, and five cities. Age comes with each person's story. Gender, race, and city were assigned by a seeded draw balanced on VI‑SPDAT score. Every race and every city has 20 people (10 women, 10 men), and every race × city pair has 2 women and 2 men, so each group has a similar mix of need. Pregnant profiles are women.

VI‑SPDAT. Version 2.0 for single adults: 17 items, one point each. Scores 0–3 recommend no housing intervention (Low), 4–7 Rapid Re‑Housing (Medium), and 8–17 Permanent Supportive Housing (High).

Places. 35 High places (permanent supportive housing), 40 Medium places (rapid re‑housing), and 25 people who receive neither. These are the numbers VI‑SPDAT assigns to these 100 people, so both approaches fill the same places. VI‑SPDAT fills High places with the highest scores, then Medium places with the next highest.

Gemini. Gemini gives its own judgment, not a VI‑SPDAT calculation. It sees all 100 case summaries in one request and must assign every person High, Medium, or Low, using exactly 35, 40, and 25. A round is accepted only if every person is assigned once and the split is exact. The request is repeated for 10 rounds; each round lists the people in a new random order under new anonymous numbers, and no round can see another. Gemini never saw the VI‑SPDAT questions, items, rules, or scores, or the titles and IDs used on this page.

Final placement. Each person's placement on this site is their consensus over the rounds: people are ordered by their average category (High 2, Medium 1, Low 0), the top 35 are High, the next 40 Medium, and the rest Low. No tie falls on a cut-off.

Prompt sent in every round

Identical in all 10 rounds; only the order and numbering of the 100 case summaries changes.

1. Design

          
A within-subjects comparison of two ways of assigning the same 100 people to housing priority categories when places are limited. Each person receives one VI‑SPDAT assignment and ten Gemini assignments. The outcome is the category (High, Medium, or Low) each approach gives each person, compared category to category, overall and within groups. VI‑SPDAT is treated as a benchmark, not as ground truth.


          
2. Population

          
100 distinct synthetic adults, written for this study with AI assistance and reviewed by the researcher. Each has an age, a gender, a race, a city, and an eleven-section case summary (work and education, path into homelessness, current housing, finances, health, safety, family and social ties, a typical day, goals, service history, and presentation in the interview). Every VI‑SPDAT item is stated outright, yes or no, so neither approach has to infer it.

          
Two genders (female, male), five races (American Indian, Alaska Native, or Indigenous; Asian or Asian American; Black, African American, or African; Hispanic/Latino/e/a; White), and five cities (Los Angeles, Cincinnati, Chicago, Atlanta, New York City). Every race and every city has 20 people (10 women, 10 men), and every race by city pair has 2 women and 2 men. The assignment was a seeded draw balanced on VI‑SPDAT score, so each group has a similar mix of need. Pregnancy appears only in women. Generation was checked automatically for leakage of scores or categories and for explicit coverage of every item.


          
3. VI‑SPDAT benchmark

          
VI‑SPDAT 2.0 for single adults: 17 items scored one point each, total 0 to 17. The published bands are Low (0 to 3, no housing intervention), Medium (4 to 7, rapid re-housing), and High (8 to 17, permanent supportive housing). Scores were computed from each profile's coded items with no judgment involved. These bands place 35 people in High, 40 in Medium, and 25 in Low.


          
4. Limited places

          
There are 35 High places and 40 Medium places; the other 25 people receive neither. These are the numbers VI‑SPDAT's own bands assign to these 100 people, so both approaches fill identical places and differences come from who gets them. VI‑SPDAT fills the High places with the highest scores and the Medium places with the next highest; no tie falls on a cut-off.


          
5. Gemini procedure

          
Model. Gemini 3.8 Flash through the Gemini API (generate_content), default settings (default reasoning, no system instruction, default sampling). Each request is stateless: it contains the instructions, all 100 case summaries, and the reply format, and nothing else.

          
Prompt. Housing resources are limited. The model is told the three categories and how many places each has, and must assign every applicant to exactly one category using exactly 35 High, 40 Medium, and 25 Low. The categories are defined by the kind of help (intensive long-term, time-limited, or little or no help), the same way as VI‑SPDAT's recommendations. The model never sees VI‑SPDAT questions, items, rules, or scores, or the case titles and profile IDs. It does see each person's age, gender, race, and city, which is what makes demographic comparisons meaningful. The full prompt is on the Study design page.

          
Rounds. The request was repeated 10 times. In each round the 100 people appear in a new random order under new anonymous applicant numbers (seeded shuffle), so a person's result cannot depend on a fixed position and no round can see another. A reply is accepted only if it is valid JSON, assigns every applicant exactly once, and uses exactly 35, 40, and 25; otherwise it is saved separately and the round is asked again. All 10 main-scenario rounds were accepted on the first attempt.

          
Final placement. To report one placement per person, people are ranked by their average category over the 10 rounds (High 2, Medium 1, Low 0); the top 35 are High, the next 40 Medium, and the rest Low. No tie falls on a cut-off. Results are also shown per round, and the Robustness page compares alternative ways of combining rounds.


          
6. Statistical analysis

          
Agreement. The proportion of people placed in the same category, with a Wilson 95% interval; linear-weighted Cohen's kappa between the two sets of ordered categories, with a bootstrap interval (2,000 resamples of people); Spearman's rho and Kendall's tau between the VI‑SPDAT score and Gemini's average category. People placed higher or lower than VI‑SPDAT are counted separately.

          
Reliability across rounds. Fleiss' kappa treating the 10 rounds as 10 raters of 100 people, and the number of people given the same category in every pair of rounds.

          
Who gets High places. For each circumstance and demographic factor, the outcome is each person's share of rounds placed High. The test asks whether the group's High-place rate differs from what people with the same VI‑SPDAT score would have: labels are shuffled within VI‑SPDAT score (stratified permutation test, 20,000 shuffles). Holm correction is applied within each family (16 circumstances; 4 demographic factors). Because VI‑SPDAT score is held fixed, a significant result means the factor changes Gemini's choice beyond what VI‑SPDAT's score already says.

          
Factor weights. Ordinary least squares of each person's High-place share on the 17 VI‑SPDAT factors, with HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors and Holm-adjusted p-values. VI‑SPDAT gives every factor one point; the regression shows how Gemini weighs them. Exploratory, because factors co-occur and 100 people limit precision.

          
Uncertainty and robustness. Group differences carry bootstrap 95% intervals (resampling people within the group). Each permutation test was repeated on each round alone to see how consistent significance is. Minimum detectable differences were computed for the group sizes in this study.


          
7. Reproducibility

          
Every round is saved with its exact prompt, the order and numbering of the people, the reply, the model version, token counts, and a SHA-256 hash of the prompt. The scripts that build the profiles, score VI‑SPDAT, run the rounds, and produce every figure are in the repository (pilot/scripts/), and the data files are linked on the Downloads page. Random seeds are fixed in the scripts.

Measurement and design

          
    
            
  • Synthetic people. The case summaries are written, not observed. They state every VI‑SPDAT item outright, which removes the ambiguity real intake interviews have. Results show how each approach treats this kind of description and should not be read as outcomes for real applicants.
    • 
            
  • VI‑SPDAT is a benchmark, not ground truth. The study compares Gemini with VI‑SPDAT. It cannot say which allocation leads to better outcomes for real people; no housing outcomes are observed.
    • 
            
  • Capacity was chosen to match VI‑SPDAT. The 35 and 40 places equal the numbers VI‑SPDAT's bands give these people. This guarantees comparable allocations but means VI‑SPDAT fills the places exactly; real shortages are often more severe.
    • 
            
  • Demographics were assigned to stories. Gender, race, and city were assigned by a balanced draw, so they are independent of need by design. This supports demographic comparisons but cannot show how the approaches behave where need and demographics are correlated in real populations. Gender has two categories only, and intersectional groups (for example race by gender) are too small to analyze.
    • 
            
  • Gemini sees demographics. This is intended, since the question is whether they change its choices, but it means any demographic effect could come from the model's learned associations.
    • 
          

          
Statistics

          
    
            
  • Small groups. Groups range from 9 to 50 people (races and cities have 20). Only large differences can be detected; the Robustness page shows the smallest detectable difference for each group size.
    • 
            
  • Multiple tests. Sixteen circumstances and four demographic factors were tested. Holm correction was applied within each family, and results significant only before correction (for example, risk of exploitation and substance use) are suggestive, not established.
    • 
            
  • Repeated rounds are not independent samples of people. The 10 rounds measure Gemini's variability on the same 100 people, not new people. Intervals describe uncertainty about these people's placements; generalizing to other people requires a larger sample.
    • 
            
  • Consensus placement. One placement per person comes from averaging the rounds. Other rules give similar agreement, and per-round results are reported, but the consensus is a summary choice.
    • 
            
  • Regression is exploratory. The 17 factors often occur together (for example, tri-morbidity combines three of them), so single factor weights are imprecise. The permutation tests, which hold the VI‑SPDAT score fixed, are the primary evidence.
    • 
          

          
Model

          
    
            
  • One model and one prompt. Results are for Gemini 3.8 Flash with a single prompt wording. Other models, versions, or wordings may behave differently, and Gemini versions change over time.
    • 
            
  • Default sampling and reasoning. Settings were left at the model's defaults, so answers vary between rounds. This is reported (Fleiss' kappa 0.83 across rounds) rather than hidden by fixing a seed, which the API does not guarantee.
    • 
            
  • One long request. All 100 people are assigned in one request, which is what makes the allocation a real trade-off but means long-context effects, such as attention to list position, are possible. The position check on the Robustness page addresses this.
    • 
          

        

      


      

        

          

            
VI‑SPDAT
The Vulnerability Index and Service Prioritization Decision Assistance Tool, a 17-item survey scored 0 to 17 that many US homeless services use to prioritize housing. Here, version 2.0 for single adults.

            
Priority category
High (permanent supportive housing, intensive long-term help), Medium (rapid re-housing, time-limited help), or Low (little or no help). VI‑SPDAT's bands are 8 to 17, 4 to 7, and 0 to 3.

            
Places
The fixed number of High (35) and Medium (40) slots available. The other 25 people receive neither.

            
Round
One request to Gemini containing all 100 people in a new random order. Ten rounds were run.

            
Consensus placement
A person's single placement after combining the rounds: people are ranked by their average category, the top 35 are High, the next 40 Medium, and the rest Low.

            
Percentage points
The difference between two percentages. Going from 50% to 60% is a difference of 10 percentage points.

            
Weighted Cohen's kappa
Agreement between two sets of ordered categories beyond what chance would give; 0 is chance, 1 is perfect. Disagreeing by two categories counts more than by one. Roughly, 0.61 to 0.80 is substantial and above 0.80 almost perfect.

            
Fleiss' kappa
The same idea for more than two raters. Here the 10 rounds are 10 raters, so it measures how consistently Gemini places the same people.

            
Spearman's rho, Kendall's tau
Rank correlations between the VI‑SPDAT score and Gemini's average category. 1 means the two rank people identically; 0 means unrelated.

            
Permutation test
Shuffle a label (for example, who has a circumstance) many times to see how large a difference chance alone produces, then ask how unusual the real difference is.

            
Stratified
Shuffling only among people with the same VI‑SPDAT score, so the test asks whether a factor matters beyond the score.

            
p-value
How often a difference at least this large would occur by chance if the factor made no difference. Small values suggest a real difference.

            
Holm correction
An adjustment for running many tests at once, so the chance of any false alarm stays at 5%. Sort the p-values from smallest; multiply the smallest by the number of tests, the next by one fewer, and so on.

            
Bootstrap confidence interval
A range found by resampling the people many times; the true value is plausibly inside it. A 95% interval that includes zero is consistent with no difference.

            
Wilson interval
A confidence interval for a proportion, such as 78 of 100.

            
HC3 standard errors
Standard errors for a regression that stay valid when the spread of the data is uneven, which suits small samples.

            
Minimum detectable difference
The smallest difference a study of this size would detect 80% of the time. Smaller differences cannot be told from chance.

            
Sankey (alluvial) diagram
A chart where bands connect the groups people start in to the groups they end in; band width is the number of people.
