Algorithmic Bias in Homeless Vulnerability Assessment

Comparing the VI‑SPDAT scoring algorithm with an AI‑based substitute on an identical synthetic population, to test whether either one shifts a person's vulnerability score by race or gender.

Status  Design complete, data collection pending Instrument  VI‑SPDAT (OrgCode, 2015) Analysis  Paired t‑test, α = 0.05
01

Background and why it matters

I want to study the underlying bias within the algorithms used for the Coordinated Entry System (CES), which assesses and prioritizes the most vulnerable unhoused individuals for shelters, need‑based assistance, and funding. Trained staff evaluates the severity of the situations of unhoused individuals with questions from a standardized screening tool, such as the Vulnerability Index–Service Prioritization Decision Assistance Tool (VI‑SPDAT), which is frequently selected on the CoC level in the United States. According to the Coordinated Entry System Triage Tool Research and Refinement, more than one out of four CoCs report using a VI‑SPDAT. After in‑person assessments, the VI‑SPDAT algorithm outputs a criticality score based on the subject's answers. Lastly, the participants are ranked based on their score for organizational support.

Why this is a problem

Even though the system intended to fairly address vulnerable homeless populations, several studies, such as “CES Racial Equity Analysis of Assessment Data” by C4 Innovations, a national research organization, have reported that the VI‑SPDAT system has been persistently biased against people of color. For instance, a Black man may rank under a white man in the homeless priority system even if he needed more help than him, because of the intrinsic bias in VI‑SPDAT, a human‑created algorithm. This partiality may leave some of the most vulnerable people without any assistance, misallocating scarce, important resources to less vulnerable groups. In December of 2020, VI‑SPDAT's creator, OrgCode, issued an announcement that addressed the algorithm's racial and sexual inaccuracies, and even encouraged users to substitute VI‑SPDAT with newer, less biased systems. Currently, a few CoCs are transitioning into a machine learning (ML)‑based system as a replacement for VI‑SPDAT. However, there is not prevalent research explaining that ML based algorithms would increase the fairness of the overall system.

This research would connect well to the overarching group theme as it assesses the bias of human‑made technology and an AI‑based replacement on a similar data sample, which heavily impacts the resource distribution for homeless populations. This means that it relates directly to the inequality that marginalized communities face as a result of inaccurate technology.

02

Research question

When analyzing the same set of client data, how do AI‑based models perform compared with VI‑SPDAT?

This project would evaluate the result for bias and accuracy against the actual vulnerability of these individuals.

Hypothesis

My hypothesis is that the AI‑based model would outperform VI‑SPDAT in terms of accuracy, but still remain slightly skewed against people of color.

04

Method

To answer my research question, I would construct a synthetic population and test both the published VI‑SPDAT model and an AI‑based LLM on it, prompting both models to find the vulnerability for each of the individuals. For the synthetic population, I would create around 32 base profiles based on the 16 vulnerability indicators of the VI‑SPDAT model to diversify the synthetic population. Then, I would clone these profiles across two different genders and five different races according to the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD)'s race and ethnicity categories.

Since some individuals withhold their answers to certain questions due to fear of being judged, I would test each profile twice—once with the individual truthfully answering all questions, and another with them refusing to answer or underdisclosing occasionally. If this proves significant, I would cite research about how race and gender may contribute to the withholding of information.

After each model outputs a vulnerability score, I would examine if the score changed across each base profile. If no bias is present, the score for one base profile should stay the same no matter the person's gender or race. Lastly, I would use a paired t‑test to assess if the difference reached the critical value at the 0.05 level (95%). If it did, I would be able to conclude that the model is biased.

Run protocol

Scoring 640 instances in sequence would let a model notice that it is re-reading the same case with the demographics swapped. The run is therefore split into 20 sessions, each started in a fresh context, and built so that no two clones of the same base profile are ever scored together.

Design note — how the schedule is built

Two constraints have to hold at once: no session may contain two clones of one base profile, and every base profile must still be seen under all 20 conditions. Together these give a cyclic Latin square. Base profile i in session s takes condition (i + s) mod 20, so each profile appears exactly once per session and cycles through every condition across the 20 sessions. Presentation order is then shuffled inside each session, so position in the run cannot be confounded with condition.

The schedule is generated from a fixed seed and verified before it is written: the generator asserts that no session repeats a profile, that positions run 1–32 with no gaps, and that every profile covers all 20 conditions exactly once. It will refuse to write a schedule that breaks the design.

05

The 32 base profiles

Each base profile is a demographically neutral case description recorded at the indicator level: which of the 16 vulnerability indicators, plus the pre‑survey age indicator, are flagged. Domain subtotals and the total score are computed from those flags using the published scoring rules rather than assigned by hand, so no profile can carry a score its own answers do not produce.

These are the profiles that get cloned across every gender and race combination, so the profile text carries no name, pronoun, or other demographic signal—the only thing that changes between a profile's ten clones is the stated gender and race.

Each profile also carries a work history, a financial picture, a path into homelessness, a daily routine, a service record, and a description of how the person presents in the interview. Expand any row below to read them.

Design note — why the profiles are this detailed

The depth is methodological rather than decorative. In a thin profile, the stated race and gender are the only features distinguishing one clone from another, which makes the manipulation obvious to a language model and invites it either to refuse the comparison or to overcorrect for it. That is the risk raised in the limitations below: that the model notices what is being tested and returns identical scores by design rather than by fairness.

Surrounded by eight fields of unrelated detail, the demographic line becomes one item among many rather than the obvious variable. Every added detail is held identical across all ten clones of a profile, so it cannot produce a demographic difference on its own, and each field is screened for gendered, racial, national, and religious wording — a detail that carried its own demographic signal would reintroduce exactly the confound the detail is meant to mask.

How the profiles are distributed

Where the 32 base profiles fall on the 0–17 scale

Every total from 0 to 17 is represented, and the two decision thresholds are deliberately oversampled — three profiles score 3 and three score 4; three score 7 and three score 8 — because that is where a one‑point demographic shift changes which intervention a person is referred to.

Each bar counts how many of the 32 base profiles score that exact total. Band thresholds follow the published VI‑SPDAT recommendation ranges: 0–3 no housing intervention, 4–7 an assessment for Rapid Re‑Housing, 8+ an assessment for Permanent Supportive Housing / Housing First.

Design note

Profiles sharing a total score are given different domain compositions on purpose. P20 and P23 both score in the same band but load onto different domains, and P30, P31 and P32 reach 16, 16 and 17 by different routes. Because VI‑SPDAT is purely additive, it cannot distinguish them; an LLM might. That difference is itself a testable result.

Tri‑morbidity is treated as a derived indicator, flagged only where physical health, substance use, and mental health are all flagged, exactly as the tool specifies. The data file is validated against every domain maximum and against this rule.

Planned results

The VI‑SPDAT arm has been run. All 640 instances were scored with the published additive algorithm; the AI arm has not been run yet, and its columns below are still empty.

Result — VI‑SPDAT arm

VI‑SPDAT produced identical scores across every race and gender. Mean score was 6.406 for all five HUD race categories and for both genders; every paired t‑test on race and gender returned a mean difference of exactly 0.000. This is not a null result to be explained away — it follows from the algorithm's structure. Race is not an input to any VI‑SPDAT scoring rule, and the only gender‑dependent item in version 2.0 is the pregnancy question, which no base profile sets.

The documented racial bias in VI‑SPDAT therefore cannot live in the arithmetic. It has to enter through the interview: which questions get asked, how answers are interpreted and recorded, and who feels safe disclosing what. That makes the disclosure condition the more revealing half of this arm.

Withholding stigmatized information cost 1.94 points on average (t = −22.47, p < 0.001) — and moved 100 of 320 profile-and-demographic combinations into a lower triage band, changing the intervention they would be referred to. If disclosure varies by race or gender in real interviews, that is a mechanism large enough to produce the disparities reported in the literature without a single biased line of arithmetic.

07

Expected contribution and limitations

I expect this study to improve our understanding on the limitations and strengths of AI in terms of fairly scoring vulnerable and marginalized individuals to be used as a tool to reduce demographic bias. A limitation in my study is that the tested population is synthetic, meaning that it may have less variation than an actual population. I am not certain if this systematic distribution of conditions and traits would alter the results. For instance, demographic bias in real life tests may cluster around scores 3–4 out of 17, while my synthetic base profiles mostly score 11–12.

Design note

The profile set in section 05 was built to answer this last concern directly. Rather than concentrating in the 11–12 range, it covers every total from 0 to 17, and it deliberately oversamples the two decision thresholds — the 3/4 boundary and the 7/8 boundary — where a one‑point shift changes which intervention a person is referred to. Profiles that share a total score are also given different domain compositions, which makes it possible to check whether a model responds to which domain drives a score rather than to the total alone.

Limitations

  • Since I do not have access to the actual ML models used, I would need to mimic its results by using a LLM. The results of this study may not be able to be interpolated in ML models.
  • 32 base profiles may not be varied enough to accurately make generalizations about racial and sexual bias.
  • The LLM may adapt to the clones of the base profiles and just give me the same scores across races and gender. It may identify that race and gender do not have an impact in homeless severity.
